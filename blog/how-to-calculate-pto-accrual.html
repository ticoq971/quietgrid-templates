How to calculate PTO accrual (with worked examples)

PTO accrual is simply how fast paid time off is earned. There are four common methods. Here's how each works, with numbers you can check and spreadsheet formulas you can copy.

1. Annual allowance, front-loaded

The whole allowance is available on the first day of the plan year. It's the simplest method to run. The only calculation is pro-rating for people who join mid-year:

prorated days = yearly allowance × months remaining (including hire month) ÷ 12

Example: 20 days a year, hired on July 15. Counting July to December gives 6 months, so 20 × 6 ÷ 12 = 10 days for the first year. Some companies count from the first full month instead (5 months → 8.33 days). Pick one rule and write it down.

2. Monthly accrual

Each month, employees earn 1/12 of the yearly allowance.

accrued = yearly allowance ÷ 12 × months worked in the year

Example: 20 days a year → 1.67 days per month. After April (4 months): 20 ÷ 12 × 4 = 6.67 days. In a spreadsheet:

=ROUND(Allowance/12*Months, 2)

To get "months elapsed" for the current year automatically: =MONTH(TODAY()) (when accrual is credited at the start of each month), or =MONTH(TODAY())-1 (when it's credited at the end).

3. Per pay period

4. Per hour worked

Typical for part-time or hourly staff: a fixed amount of PTO for every hour worked.

accrued hours = hours worked × accrual rate

Example: 1 hour of PTO per 30 hours worked (rate 1/30 ≈ 0.0333). A 40-hour week earns 40 ÷ 30 = 1.33 hours. California's paid sick leave law, for example, uses 1 hour per 30 hours worked as its minimum accrual rate.

Carry-over and caps

If your policy lets unused days roll over up to a cap, the amount carried into next year is:

=MIN(Cap, MAX(0, Remaining))

Example: 7 unused days with a 5-day cap → 5 days carry over.

Remaining balance

remaining = accrued + carry-over − days taken (approved)

Count "days taken" in working days, not calendar days. A Monday-to-Friday week off is 5 days, not 7. See how to count working days excluding holidays.

Check your local rules

Accrual rules are partly set by law, and this guide isn't legal advice. Two examples: the EU Working Time Directive guarantees at least four weeks of paid annual leave, and California doesn't allow "use-it-or-lose-it" vacation policies (although it allows reasonable caps on accrual). Check the rules for your country or state and for your contracts.
